# Unit 1 — The Nine Parts of Speech

A self-paced unit a student can work through alone, built on one Edgar Allan Poe
short story. It teaches each part of speech and then checks recall with
interactive questions, one screen at a time.

Open it at **🎓 Start the unit** on the home page, from the Example library, or
directly at `#/study/pos`.

## What it is

**A fourth mode, for students.** Edit, Present, and Practice are all built around
a teacher: someone chooses a passage, labels it, and drives. The unit is the
opposite — a student opens it and works, with no teacher, no login, and no
network. It is the first piece of Sentence Forge that sequences anything *above* a
single lesson.

Each stop follows the same rhythm:

1. **A teach screen** — what this part of speech does, then each kind of it with a
   plain-language definition, an example, and the colour the app uses for it.
2. **Recall questions** on what that screen just said.
3. **Applied questions** on the real passage: *"Select the possessive noun in this
   sentence."* The student clicks or keyboards to the word and presses Check.
4. **A sorting question**, on the reviews and the capstone: several words from the
   passage and one box per part of speech. **Tap a word, then tap where it goes** —
   there is no dragging anywhere in this unit, deliberately, because dragging is
   the thing most likely to fail on a school tablet. Tap a word you have already
   placed to take it back out.
5. **A results screen** naming what to look at again.

The whole unit runs about **five hours** — roughly a two-week independent study.
Individual stops are 20–25 minutes, which is the unit a student can finish in one
sitting.

**The passage is Poe's "The Cask of Amontillado" (1846).** One story across every
stop, so the language gets familiar while the grammar changes. It was chosen for a
practical reason as much as a literary one: it actually contains a real example of
every kind of word the unit teaches — Fortunato and Luchesi for proper nouns, *"a
great and numerous family"* for a collective noun, *"the river's bed"* for a
possessive, *"neither by word nor deed"* for a correlative conjunction. A 9th-grade
staple, and public domain.

## Scope and sequence

Fifteen stops in four clusters. Nouns come first because determiners introduce
them and pronouns replace them; the modifiers come after the things they modify;
interjections are last because they are the one part of speech that sits *outside*
the sentence, which is a useful thing to say at the end and a confusing thing to
say at the start.

| # | Stop | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Orientation** | All nine at once — the idea that a part of speech is a *job*, not a kind of word |
| | **Cluster A — Words that name** | |
| 1 | Nouns | common · proper · collective · abstract · concrete · possessive |
| 2 | Determiners | a · an · the · this · those — articles and the rest |
| 3 | Pronouns | personal · possessive · reflexive · relative · demonstrative · interrogative · indefinite |
| 4 | *Review A* | the three together, including *this* as adjective vs pronoun |
| | **Cluster B — The word that acts** | |
| 5 | Verbs | action · linking · helping · transitive · intransitive · modal · regular · irregular |
| 6 | *Review B* | every kind of verb, mixed |
| | **Cluster C — Words that modify** | |
| 7 | Adjectives | descriptive · proper · demonstrative · possessive · quantity · comparative · superlative |
| 8 | Adverbs | manner · time · place · frequency · degree |
| 9 | *Review C* | the two together, and the words that look like both |
| | **Cluster D — Words that connect and exclaim** | |
| 10 | Prepositions | place, time, direction |
| 11 | Conjunctions | coordinating · subordinating · correlative |
| 12 | Interjections | the part of speech outside the sentence |
| 13 | *Review D* | the three together |
| 14 | **Capstone** | all nine, on the closing paragraphs — text no lesson has used |

**Navigation is open.** Any stop can be opened at any time; finished ones are
ticked and the next unstarted one is highlighted. Nothing is locked, on purpose —
a student working alone has nobody to unstick them, and a teacher may want to
assign stop 7 directly.

All fifteen stops are complete and playable.

## The capstone

Stop 14 is the only one that is **an assessment rather than a lesson**, and it is
built differently on purpose.

- **No teaching.** There are no teach screens at all. It opens on its first
  question.
- **Text the student has not seen.** The passage is drawn from the story's closing
  paragraphs, and not one of its sentences appears in any of the nine lessons or
  four reviews. That is the only thing that shows the unit taught something
  transferable rather than something memorised, and it is a checked property of the
  app, not an intention.
- **About thirty questions, mostly *find the word*.** Roughly thirty questions
  spread across all nine parts of speech, weighted towards finding one in a real
  sentence rather than reciting a definition — because that is the skill that
  survives the unit. It ends with two sorting questions that between them use all
  nine boxes.
- **Results by cluster, not by question.** The results screen reads *"Words that
  modify — 4 of 7"* with a link straight back into Review C, rather than listing
  thirty rights and wrongs. Thirty rows tell a student nothing they can do; four
  named clusters point at a stop they can re-open in one click.

Expect about **30 minutes**. It is the last stop, but it is not a gate — nothing
before it depends on it, and a student can take it twice.

## What it teaches — and what it doesn't

The app defines **54** part-of-speech labels. The unit teaches **48** of them: the
nine bases and 39 kinds. The other six are left out deliberately.

**Held back for a later unit — `gerund`, `participle`, `infinitive`.** These are
verb *forms* doing some other part of speech's job: *Skating is risky* uses a verb
form as a noun. That is really a phrase-level idea, and the app already has
`gerund-phrase`, `participial-phrase`, and `infinitive-phrase` waiting on the
Phrases layer. Teaching them inside a parts-of-speech unit invites exactly the
confusion the unit is trying to prevent. Where the story contains one, it is
labelled simply as a verb.

**Left out as advanced — `particle`, `relative-adverb`, `emphatic-pronoun`.**
These carry the app's *Advanced* tier. Every unit passage has **Essential only**
switched on, so a teacher who opens one gets the classroom-core palette.

Full definitions for all 87 labels are in
[grammar-reference.md](grammar-reference.md).

## Assigning it

Nothing to provision. Three ways, in order of how little you have to do:

1. **Send the link.** Post the app's address plus `#/study/pos` in Google
   Classroom. Works on a school machine with no login.
2. **Point at the home page.** The unit has a card on it, so "open Sentence Forge
   and start Unit 1" is a complete instruction.
3. **Hand out the files.** The unit's passages are also ordinary lessons in the
   **🎓 Unit 1** section of the Example library. Add one to your lessons and you
   can **Present** it, **Edit** it, or build a printed
   [worksheet](teacher-guide.md) from it like any other passage.

If you want the passages without the unit, that third route is the one — the
Poe lessons stand on their own.

## What a teacher can and cannot see

**Nothing.** This is worth being blunt about, because it is a design decision
rather than a missing feature.

A student's progress is a **resume point and a best score per lesson, stored in
that one browser on that one machine.** It is never uploaded, never attached to a
name, and never readable by anyone else. No per-question record is kept even
locally, so there is no way — for us or for you — to reconstruct what a student
answered.

That is what lets the unit need no accounts, no permission slips, and no data
agreement. **If you need evidence a student did the work, use the printed
worksheet and answer key** ([teacher-guide.md](teacher-guide.md)); that path is
designed for it and this one is not.

## Limits worth knowing before you assign it

Each of these will otherwise be discovered mid-class.

- **Progress lives in one browser on one machine.** The same constraint that
  applies to lessons. A student who moves to a different computer, a different
  browser, or a cleared profile starts over. A unit is short enough that this is
  survivable, but say so up front.
- **A shared machine hands the next student the last one's progress.** There are no
  accounts to keep them apart. That is why **Reset my progress** sits on the unit
  map rather than buried — point it out to a class working on a cart.
- **Selecting words is whole-word only,** and punctuation belongs to the word it is
  attached to. A student clicking *"river's"* gets the whole token, which is
  correct.
- **Drag-select on a tablet is the least-tested part of the app.** Clicking a
  single word works, and so does the keyboard path (Tab to a word, then Enter, or
  Shift+Arrow to extend). If a class is on iPads, teach the click. The sorting
  questions never use dragging at all, for exactly this reason.
- **Every question can be answered from the keyboard.** Tab reaches every word,
  option, and sorting box; Enter or Space activates it; the arrow keys walk a row
  of words or boxes. Nothing in the unit needs a mouse.
- **The score you see is the best score, not the latest.** Re-taking a stop can
  only improve what the map shows, which is deliberate — a student experimenting
  with a stop they have already passed cannot lose anything by it.
- **The unit does not teach sentence structure** — no subjects, predicates,
  phrases, or clauses. That is what Present mode and a later unit are for.
- **The story is about a murder.** Poe's narrator walls a man up alive. It is
  standard 9th-grade reading and the unit quotes it without comment, but it is
  worth knowing what you have assigned.
